Wellmatix · Platform Validation · Case Study 01

Target-Specific Binder Validation and Ranking Correction

A blinded benchmark, built to score the platform against a literature panel, was turned back on the platform itself. It found that a scoring objective had been measuring the behaviour of a database search rather than a property of the design — and changed which candidate the platform recommends.

What this document reports

Summary

The platform screens a surface proteome, gates candidates on normal-tissue risk, retrieves candidate binders, assembles constructs and ranks what survives. Binder retrieval works by querying the RCSB Protein Data Bank on the target's UniProt accession, and reading antibody annotation for the entries it returns from SAbDab, the structural antibody database. A hit means the deposited entry contains the target. It does not mean the antibody in that entry was raised against it.

A validation check introduced for an independent benchmark was applied to the platform's own pool. It compares the antigen a depositor recorded against every name the target's UniProt record gives it. Across the pancreatic pool it flagged 83 of 315 structural binder rows, distributed across 25 of 200 candidate targets.

One of those candidates was a design the platform was recommending for advancement. Its entire claim to that recommendation was a single retrieved binder — annotated against a G-protein subunit rather than against the receptor. The scoring objective has been corrected to count target-matched binders, and that recommendation has been withdrawn.

Three numbers, three different populations

83 of 315 counts binder rows and is the check's raw output. 64 of 315 is what survives once the rows the check itself misread are removed — see The check's own failure mode below, which gives the derivation and corrects an earlier estimate of 57 rows. 25 of 200 candidate targets counts targets, and is the figure that bears on what the platform recommends, because recommendations are made about targets rather than about rows; the corrected candidate figure is 21 of 200 candidate targets.

The row-level rate is the higher of the two in this pool, because the affected targets happen to carry several flagged binders each. That is a fact about these data and not an arithmetic necessity: had each affected target carried exactly one flagged binder, the row rate would have been the lower of the two. Each figure is therefore quoted with its unit and its population named throughout, and quoting any one of them alone would misstate the finding.

The blinded benchmark, and how the blind was enforced

Method

The check originated in a benchmark exercise: score the platform's binder retrieval and ranking against a panel of literature-validated answers for a well-characterised target. A benchmark scored after the answers are known measures nothing, so the blind was enforced mechanically rather than by undertaking.

The comparison tool refuses to run unless the frozen platform output was committed to version control before the answer panel was. Four independent layers stand behind that refusal, each catching something the others would not:

  • The import graph is walked by parsing, not by importing. Reading a module to see what it depends on must not execute it, or the act of checking could itself pull the answers into scope.
  • Held-out values are scanned for across every reachable module. Catches an answer transcribed into code by hand, which an import graph cannot see.
  • The frozen output is fingerprinted. Catches a result edited after the fact to agree with the panel.
  • The commit graph is the evidence of ordering. Catches the case the other three cannot: output produced honestly, but only after the answers were visible.

The ordering layer is the load-bearing one, and it is the one that cannot be satisfied by assertion. The frozen output was committed on 2026-09-07; the answer panel was committed after it, in a later commit on the same day. The guard resolves both commits and confirms the frozen artifact does not come later.

What the ordering layer does not prove

It proves the frozen output existed before the answers entered the repository. It does not prove nobody had seen the literature by other means. No mechanism can prove that, and the check is written to say so rather than to imply otherwise.

Outside a version-controlled copy the question cannot be asked at all. In that situation the guard reports that the ordering is not evaluable — it does not report a pass. A distributed archive carries no history, and treating absent history as a satisfied condition would turn the strongest layer into the weakest.

What the platform was standing on

Source coverage

The pancreatic run screened a surface proteome and carried 200 candidate targets into binder retrieval. Retrieval runs two independent routes, reported separately and never summed: a structural route that queries the RCSB Protein Data Bank on the target's UniProt accession and reads antibody annotation for the returned entries from SAbDab, and a sequence route that matches named therapeutic antibodies recorded against the target in Thera-SAbDab.

Nine datasets are cached and version-pinned: the reviewed human proteome from UniProt (20,431 entries), normal-tissue, pathology and subcellular expression from the Human Protein Atlas, bulk normal-tissue medians from GTEx, CRISPR gene-effect scores from DepMap, the SAbDab structural summary together with Thera-SAbDab therapeutics as one antibody dataset, gene span annotation, trial counts per antigen, construct part sequences, and the anti-tag binder sequence every adaptor design is built on. The RCSB Protein Data Bank is not among them: it is queried directly at retrieval time, by exact UniProt accession cross-reference rather than by name.

Of the 200, 192 were blocked on normal-tissue risk before binder retrieval mattered, and 3 more were dropped for retrieving no binder at all. Five designs reached the ranking.

What the five designs are, and why four of them carry no binder

The ranked table further down shows four of those five with a retrieved binder count of zero, which appears to contradict a gate that just dropped three candidates for retrieving no binder. The reconciling fact is the receptor architecture, and it needs stating before any of the numbers can be read.

All five designs route to an adaptor architecture. The receptor binds a peptide tag carried on a separately dosed adaptor molecule, and the adaptor carries the tumour-targeting moiety; the receptor never binds the antigen directly. Such a design assembles without a target-specific binder in the construct, so it clears the retrieval gate on a different basis from a conventional single-antigen receptor. The three candidates dropped at that gate had neither a retrieved binder nor an admissible adaptor route.

Two things follow that a reader needs. Binder count still matters for these designs, because the adaptor itself has to be built against the target and a binder is the evidence that one can be — which is why an objective counting binders that are not against the target was worth correcting. And the safety margins throughout this document are negative for a reason that is architectural rather than alarming; that is set out where the objectives are defined.

The finding, and what causes it

Validation result

The check asks a deliberately narrow question: does the antigen the depositors recorded name the target we searched for? It does not ask what the antibody binds. Answering that needs the deposited coordinates, which the platform does not have connected, and the check is explicit that it is not answering it.

Across 315 structural rows the verdicts divide cleanly, and the three states sum to the population:

The dominant cause is structural, and systematic

The largest group is not scattered error. G-protein-coupled receptors are frequently crystallised as a receptor–G-protein complex, often with an antibody fragment used to stabilise the assembly. A search on the receptor's accession finds that entry correctly. The antibody inside it is annotated against the G-protein subunits.

21 of the flagged rows record the same G-protein beta-1 subunit as their antigen, across seven unrelated receptor targets. Further groups record G-protein alpha subunits. The pattern reproduces wherever the receptor class does.

A second, quite different group is crystallisation machinery: maltodextrin-binding fusion partners, immunoglobulin-binding constructs, enterotoxin chains. These are reagents that made an experiment possible, counted by a naive retrieval step as though they were therapeutic candidates.

The pattern is not an artefact of one indication. Run against the platform's second indication — invasive breast carcinoma, an entirely separate pool and screen — the same check flags 23% of structural rows against 26% here, with the same two causes dominating. A retrieval step that counts entries containing the target behaves this way wherever it is pointed.

Breast: 46 flagged of 200 structural rows. The 200 there is a count of rows and is unrelated to the 200 candidate targets in the pancreatic pool above — the totals coincide and the populations do not.

The 83 is an upper bound, not a result

19 of the 83 flagged rows name the target itself, in a form the check's comparison could not see. The genuine figure is 64 of 315. The mechanism, the derivation, and a correction to an earlier estimate that was too low, are set out in The check's own failure mode, below, after the example that exposed it.

Worked example: a recommendation that did not survive

GPR35

Of the five designs that reached the ranking, four retrieved no binder at all. The fifth retrieved exactly one.

GPR35 · Q9HC97

G protein-coupled receptor 35 (kynurenic acid receptor)

One structural binder retrieved, from a single deposited entry. The entry records its antigens as:

Guanine nucleotide-binding protein subunit alpha-13
Guanine nucleotide-binding protein G(I)/G(S)/G(T) subunit beta-1

GPR35 is not listed as an antigen anywhere in its own entry. The entry was found by searching on the GPR35 accession and does contain the receptor; the antibody in it is a fiducial against the G-protein heterotrimer. This is the receptor–G-protein pattern in its purest form.

This single binder was the entire reason GPR35 was being recommended. The ranking compares candidates on four objectives at once and emits no weighted total. A candidate advances only while no rival is at least as good as it on every objective and better on at least one. The clause about ties is load-bearing: a rival that matches a candidate everywhere and beats it in one place displaces it, so a candidate survives only by being strictly better somewhere.

Why every safety margin in this document is negative

All five designs carry a negative safety margin, and none of them failed a safety gate. The margin above is computed against the persistent ceiling of 0.15 — the limit for a receptor that stays active once infused.

These five are not that. Every one of them routes to an adaptor architecture: the receptor binds a peptide tag on a separately dosed adaptor molecule rather than binding the tumour antigen directly, so activation stops when dosing stops. They were admitted against the terminable ceiling of 0.35, which is the limit they were actually judged against. FER1L6's risk is 0.2881, giving it a residual margin of +0.1769 below the ceiling that applies to it, and that positive figure is what the scoring frame uses.

The negative number is retained in the ranking because it compares all candidates on one consistent scale. It is a comparison, not a verdict, and it is stated here so it cannot be read as one.

Against FER1L6, the other advancing design, GPR35 was:

Stated plainly, the platform's recommendation amounted to: no rival displaces this design, because one database row exists, and the antibody in that row is against a different protein. That is retrieval luck presented as evidence. Remove the row and FER1L6 matches GPR35 on both binder count and cleanliness, beats it on attractiveness and on safety margin, and displaces it — which is the tie clause doing the work.

Worked example: where the check itself reaches its limit

CLDN18

A single clean success would overstate what this check does. The second example is chosen because it shows the opposite — a candidate where the check produces the wrong verdict, and where knowing why matters more than the verdict itself.

CLDN18 · P56856

Claudin-18 · 15 binders retrieved: 4 structural, 11 named therapeutics

All four structural entries record the identical antigen:

Isoform A2 of Claudin-18

The check flags all four. The UniProt record for CLDN18 yields exactly two names — Claudin-18 and CLDN18 — and Isoform A2 of Claudin-18 matches neither by exact comparison.

The verdict is wrong. Isoform A2 is claudin-18.2, the clinically validated form of this target and the antigen of an approved therapeutic antibody. These four binders are against the target, in its most therapeutically relevant form, and the matching rule cannot see it.

The remaining 11 binders are named therapeutics recorded against CLDN18 by the sequence route. They carry no deposited antigen annotation, so there is nothing for this check to match, and each reads UNKNOWN. They count toward the objective — but they count because the check does not apply to them, not because they matched. The distinction is carried on every record for exactly this reason.

CLDN18 did not reach the ranking on this run, so no recommendation turned on these verdicts. The example is included because it is the clearest illustration of what the check is and is not: agreement between two annotations, not an inspection of an interface. When the two annotations use different vocabulary for the same molecule, the check is wrong, and it is wrong in the direction of under-counting evidence.

Why an under-counting error is the tolerable direction

A missed match withholds credit from a real binder and can only make the platform more conservative about what it recommends. A missed mismatch does the opposite: it advances a design on evidence that does not exist. Both are errors and both are being addressed, but only one of them can put a wrong recommendation in front of a reader.

The check's own failure mode

Correction to the headline figure

CLDN18 is not an isolated case, and the honest reading of the headline number depends on knowing how far it generalises.

The mechanism

The check derives a set of names for the target from its UniProt record — the recommended name, each parenthesised synonym, the note on each mature chain, and the gene symbol — and compares each recorded antigen against that set by exact string equality, after lowercasing. Anything not in the set is a mismatch.

Exact equality is defeated by three label shapes that are common in deposited structures, none of which indicates a different molecule:

The genuine figure, and an estimate that was wrong

This section first carried an estimate of 57 rows, produced by a screening test over the flagged set rather than by fixing the matcher. The matcher has since been fixed, and the measured figure is 64. The estimate is corrected here rather than replaced, because how it was wrong is more informative than the number.

The screening test reduced each flagged antigen and each reference name to lowercased words, discarding the connectives of and the and the generic words isoform and protein, then reclassified a row where every remaining word of a reference name appeared in the recorded antigen. 26 of 83 rows met that test, leaving 57 rows.

The implementation discards nothing. Dropping words that look generic is precisely what makes a tolerant matcher dangerous: drop receptor and the hepatocyte growth factor ligand becomes a match for the hepatocyte growth factor receptor, which is a different molecule, and an antibody against one is not evidence about the other. The rule the fix was built to satisfy forbids the shortcut the estimate took.

So seven rows the screening test excused stay flagged: four on ERBB2, two on MUC1 recorded as MUC1 Glycopeptide and Synthetic MUC1 glycopepide, and one on TREM2 recorded as TREM-2 stalk peptide. In each, the word left over is not a qualifier describing which form of the target is present. 19 rows were genuinely the target under a label the comparison could not see; 64 name a genuinely different molecule — G-protein subunits, enterotoxin chains, the partner chain of an integrin heterodimer, crystallisation chaperones, unrelated antibody fragments.

The direction the estimate was wrong in

The estimate understated the problem. It credited the platform's retrieval with seven fewer genuinely wrong-antigen binders than it has. A partner assessing this platform should know that the first figure published erred in the flattering direction, and that it was corrected by building the fix rather than by being challenged.

The candidate-level figure moved in the same direction and was predicted exactly: 25 of 200 affected targets falls to 21 of 200, which was the number written down before the change was made.

The sixth incident of this kind, and the first in this direction

String matching against biological names is a documented recurring failure in this project, and the record is kept precisely so that a new instance is recognised as a pattern rather than treated as bad luck. Five prior incidents are on file, and every one of them ran the other way — a match that was too permissive:

  • Subcellular-localisation phrases matched as substrings of free-text notes, so “not detected at the cell membrane” read as evidence that the protein was at the cell membrane. The test admitted the negation of its own claim.
  • A compound antibody-target field matched as a substring, which put two unrelated mucins into a third mucin's bucket and matched a claudin inside the name of a different claudin carried separately in the same table.
  • Tissue names mapped to organs by substring, which put the adrenal gland at kidney criticality and folded the kidney into the brain — each mistake moving an organ between risk tiers and silently rewriting the safety gate for every protein screened.
  • A free-text name query against the structure archive, in place of an exact accession cross-reference, which returned several hundred entries whose top hits were a bacterial RNA chaperone, a sulfur transferase and a photosystem supercomplex — a valid response carrying a plausible integer that was entirely spurious.
  • A blinding guard that enumerated modules by name, behind which thirteen modules went unexamined until it was changed to enumerate by path.

The response to those five was a standing rule: match on exact values and explicit token boundaries, never on substrings. This sixth incident is the first caused by that rule itself. Exact equality does not admit the negation of a claim, and it also does not admit an isoform of the target. The correction to the correction is not a return to substring matching — which would reintroduce every failure above — but a tolerant match that is specific about which transformations it will accept.

What this does and does not change

Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions:

  • The row-level headline figure is an upper bound. 83 of 315 is the check's raw output. 64 of 315 is the rate at which retrieval returns an antibody against a genuinely different molecule. The candidate-level figure of 25 of 200 candidate targets is similarly an upper bound, and falls to 21 of 200.
  • The ranking correction is unaffected. Not by inference — this was checked directly. Every flagged row belonging to each of the five designs that reached the ranking was examined individually. Four of the five retrieved no binder at all. GPR35's single flagged binder records two G-protein heterotrimer subunits, alpha-13 and beta-1, and neither is claudin-like, isoform-labelled, fusion-tagged or a reordering of the receptor's own name. It is a genuine mismatch, and the recommendation withdrawn on the strength of it stays withdrawn.
This has since been fixed, and the fix changed the specification

The matching error was specified as a separate change with its own acceptance criteria, rather than folded into the binder-count correction, because a correction that quietly acquires a second correction stops being reviewable as either. It has since been implemented, and the figures above are the ones measured after it.

Two of that specification's own proposals did not survive the data. It proposed accepting a reordering of a target's own words, which had to be withdrawn: across the full reference set, word order is what separates paralogues. System N amino acid transporter 1 and N-system amino acid transporter 1 use identical words and are different proteins, as do two leucine-rich repeat neuronal proteins and two sialic acid-binding lectins. It also proposed refusing matches whose surplus words were drawn from a list of dangerous ones, which is not sufficient: one cadherin-family receptor matched its neighbour on a surplus of multiple and domains. The words that separate two proteins are ordinary content words, so nothing marks them dangerous in advance and the rule now names what it accepts rather than what it refuses.

Both errors were caught before the fix shipped, by a sweep of 134,075 sibling-name pairs across 2,485 targets — every pair in the reference set whose names differ only by a trailing number — which requires that naming one never matches the other. Five of the refusal cases now in the acceptance suite were found by that sweep rather than written down in advance.

The ranking, before and after

Effect

The objective now counts a binder unless its recorded antigen names a different protein. Both the original and the corrected count are computed on every run and carried on every record, together with the ranking each produces.

The correction was scoped deliberately and narrowly, and three things were held fixed and then verified to have held:

  • The gates did not move. A design holding one flagged binder still passes the “a binder was retrieved” gate and is still ranked; it simply stops scoring a point for it. Attrition and gate outcomes are identical across all 200 candidates. Moving the gate as well would have reclassified GPR35 as awaiting evidence rather than as a backup — a materially larger claim, and not the one that was approved.
  • The weighted scorecard did not move. Binder quality is scored from the binder record, not from how many were retrieved. For these five it is unmeasured for an architectural reason rather than a data one: an adaptor design retrieves no anti-tag binder, so there is no binder in the construct to score. Every component score is identical under both objectives.
  • No estimated value was introduced. Nothing was imputed to make the corrected ranking cleaner.

Twenty-three checks govern the ranking stage, and all twenty-three passed against the corrected pipeline. Seven were written for this change, and what they require was specified in prose and committed before any of the code existed — including the prediction that GPR35 would move from advance to backup and that nothing else in the pool would change. The checks themselves are code and shipped with the implementation they test; what the history evidences is that the criteria were fixed before the result was known, which is the claim worth making and the one that is true.

Across the whole platform the acceptance suite runs 240 checks over both indications. After this change 232 pass — seven more than before it, which is exactly the number of checks the change added. The eight that do not pass are pre-existing items, five of them formally accepted with reasons on record and three open; none of them involves binder counting, and none moved as a result of this work.

A check that cannot fail proves nothing

Three of the seven assert that something did not change, and an unchanged result is the easiest thing in the world to report without having looked. Each of those three therefore re-ranks the same inputs with the counting rule swapped back to its old behaviour, and refuses to pass unless that swap is first shown to have moved the ranking. Without that guard they would pass on an identity that demonstrates nothing.

What the platform will not tell you about affinity

Declared limitation

No connected data source carries a binding affinity, dissociation constant or free-energy column for these binders. The platform therefore reports affinity as UNKNOWN on every binder, and this correction did not change that.

Two consequences follow, and both are stated rather than worked around:

  • Prediction error cannot be computed. A fold error compares a predicted affinity against a measured one. There is no measured value to compare against, so no error figure exists — it is reported as not computable, not as zero and not as a wide interval.
  • Correlation against experiment cannot be computed, for the same reason. Any correlation coefficient the platform emitted would be a correlation with something it had itself invented.

The scoring frame carries binder quality as a weighted component and leaves it unmeasured, rather than dropping it. Dropping it would silently redistribute its weight across the components that are measured, making a design scored on part of the frame look directly comparable to one scored on all of it. Instead the score is normalised over the measured subset, the unmeasured components are named individually, and no score is emitted at all where less than half the applicable frame was measured.

What a matched verdict is worth

A match means the depositor's recorded antigen agrees with the UniProt record's name for the target. It does not mean an interface has been inspected, a contact residue identified, or a binding event observed. Establishing any of that requires the deposited coordinates, which are not connected.

The corrected objective is therefore a better measurement than the one it replaced — a property of the candidate rather than of the search that found it — and still a weaker claim than binders shown to bind. The platform is explicit about which of the three it is reporting.

A claim in a document that testing caught

Secondary finding

Every stage in the pipeline emits a configuration fingerprint that incorporates the fingerprint of the stage before it. The chain exists so that two runs under materially different settings cannot be mistaken for the same experiment: change a parameter, and every downstream fingerprint changes with it.

The approved decision document for this change stated that the ranking stage's fingerprint would change under the new objective, so that no cached run under the old objective could compare as equal to a new one. That statement was false against the code.

The fingerprint was computed from seven inputs — the upstream fingerprint, the candidate list, the gate definitions, the recommendation set, the decision vocabulary, the gate-status vocabulary and the scoring fingerprint. None of them described how any objective is computed. Changing the binder objective from counting hits to counting matches would have left all seven identical and the fingerprint unmoved.

The consequence is precise and serious: two experiments producing different recommendations would have carried the same identity and compared as the same run. That is the exact failure the chain exists to prevent, arriving inside the mechanism meant to prevent it.

Two details make this worth reporting to anyone assessing the platform's engineering discipline rather than only its biology:

  • It was found by testing a claim in a document, not by reading code. The sentence asserting the fingerprint would change was treated as a hypothesis and checked. Prose in a specification is the least-tested artifact in any project, because nothing re-evaluates a sentence.
  • The existing fingerprint check would never have caught it. That check perturbs a scoring weight and confirms the fingerprint moves. It had no coverage of the objective basis, so the pipeline could have shipped with a silent cache-equality defect while a green check attested to the chain.

The objective's definition is now itself an input to the fingerprint, and a check written for this change computes the fingerprint under two different definitions and requires them to differ — rather than inspecting the code and concluding they would.

The audit trail

Evidence

The requirement placed on this correction was that the record read as a chain rather than as a corrected answer with its history removed. A record carrying only the corrected number makes the first half of the story unreproducible from the platform, and a reader would have to take the description of the error on trust.

Every candidate now carries all four links:

  1. Original evidence retrieved binder count — every row the search returned
  2. Validation finding flagged count, with the recorded antigen and the path that produced each verdict
  3. Corrected evidence counted binders — the objective the ranking is computed from
  4. Ranking change the non-dominated set and the decision, recomputed under each of the two counts

The superseded ranking is recomputed on each run from that run's own inputs, not read back from a stored answer. A remembered result would drift away from the corrected result printed beside it the first time anything upstream changed, and the comparison would quietly stop being a comparison.

What the commit history evidences

The ordering below is the claim. Reading this document, you cannot check it — the repository is private, and every statement in this section is therefore something you are being asked to take on trust until it is put in front of you:

  • The validation check and its first finding were committed together on 2026-09-07, in a commit whose message states the number of flagged rows it caught.
  • The frozen benchmark output was committed before the literature answer panel, later the same day. The ordering guard resolves both and confirms it.
  • The decision to change the objective was written up as a specification and committed on 2026-09-07 — recording the predicted outcome and the alternatives rejected, before any code changed.
  • The acceptance criteria were written down before the change was built. The specification stating them — including the predicted outcome — was committed on 2026-09-09 in a commit that contains no code at all. The implementation is a later commit the same day. The checks themselves are code, and shipped in that later commit alongside what they test, which is the weaker and accurate version of this claim: what the graph evidences is that the criteria were fixed in prose before the result existed, not that executable checks predated it.

That last ordering is the one worth checking. Criteria written after a result are not criteria; they are a description of what happened. Separating them into an earlier commit is what makes the distinction visible to someone who was not present — but only to someone who can see the commits.

What would make this section checkable, and how to ask for it

An audit trail a reader cannot reach is an assertion with extra steps, and this document should not pretend otherwise. What is described above is real and is recorded in a private repository. Under technical diligence it can be produced in three forms, each stronger than the last:

The commit log for the files named here, showing message, author date and the files each commit touched — enough to check every ordering claim in this section independently. Read access to the repository at a pinned revision, which additionally lets the acceptance suite be run and its 248 checks reproduced. The run itself, executed in front of you from the published data release, which reproduces every figure in this document from source rather than from a report.

Until one of those happens, treat this section as a description of what we say the process was, and the rest of the document as the part you can weigh on its own reasoning.

The engineering correction

Change

The rule is one clause: count a binder unless its recorded antigen names something else.

It is written as a single negative test deliberately. The positive form — count a binder whose annotation names the target — reads more naturally and is a different, worse rule. It would discard every named therapeutic retrieved by the sequence route, whose unknown verdict reflects a check that does not apply rather than any doubt about the binder. It would also discard entries carrying no antigen annotation at all, treating an absent record as evidence of a different antigen. Both are imputations against the binder, and the platform's standing rule is that missing is a third state and is never filled in.

Because three unrelated situations all produce an unknown verdict, each verdict now carries a token recording which, and the counted set is broken down by path on every record. A count resting largely on absent annotation is a materially weaker claim than one resting on annotations that matched, and the two are no longer indistinguishable in the output. CLDN18 above is exactly this case: eleven counted binders, none of them matched.

Two structural choices are worth naming for anyone evaluating how the platform is built:

  • The reference records are a required input to the ranking, not an optional one. Without them every binder reads unknown, which the counting rule admits — so a permissive default would have silently restored the old behaviour of counting everything the search returned. A caller that cannot supply them now fails loudly.
  • The superseded decision is produced by the same procedure as the current one, with membership of the non-dominated set supplied to it as an input, rather than by a second implementation of the same logic. A parallel copy would be free to drift, and the entire value of carrying both decisions is that they remain comparable.

The correction did not make the platform's recommendation stronger. It made it smaller, and the reason it is smaller is now stated on the record it came from.
